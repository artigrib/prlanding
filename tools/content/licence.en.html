Open-source hardware licences explained

A robot’s print files are a copyrighted work. The licence says what you may do with them: print, modify, share, sell. Here are the most common licences, with robots from our catalogue as examples.

We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice. The full licence text of each project always applies.

01 — Three Creative Commons licences

BY, BY-SA and NC

CC BY 4.0: credit the author

You may share and adapt the files, commercially too. The condition is to credit the author, link to the licence and indicate changes.1 Example: the Project SVRN combat robot (Shoverobotics) or the AmazingHand robot hand (Pollen Robotics). That is why every kit page names the author and the licence and links to the original files.

CC BY-SA 4.0: credit the author and keep the licence

Like BY, plus adapted works must be published under the same licence.2 Example: the Otto DIY educational robot or the Berkeley Humanoid Lite. If we modified their parts, we would publish the modified files under CC BY-SA.

NC: non-commercial only

The NC (NonCommercial) clause forbids use that is “primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation”.3 Selling prints or kits is commercial use. Example: for the 150 g vertical spinner we are still checking whether CC BY-SA or BY-NC-SA applies. That is why it is listed as “coming soon” and not for sale. We offer projects with an NC clause only with the authors’ written consent.

02 — Other licences

Apache, MIT, GPL, CERN-OHL

Many robots don’t use Creative Commons at all. Creative Commons itself does not recommend its licences for software or hardware.4 So you will often see software licences (Apache-2.0, MIT, GPL-3.0) and licences written for hardware, such as CERN-OHL. Under the OSHWA definition, an open-source hardware licence must allow manufacture and sale, including commercial use; by that definition a project with an NC clause is not open-source hardware.5

We check hardware and software separately. For example, Otto DIY has its design under CC BY-SA 4.0 and its software under GPL-3.0. The SO-101 has both under Apache-2.0.

03 — Questions

Common questions

May I sell prints of a CC BY model?

Yes. CC BY allows commercial use. You must credit the author, link to the licence and indicate what you changed. The licence does not cover trademarks or patents, so you may not present the project’s name as your own brand.

What if the model has an NC clause?

Then you may not use it commercially without the author’s permission, which includes selling prints or kits. Non-commercial use, such as printing for yourself, is allowed. If in doubt, ask the author.

Do I have to publish my modifications?

Not under CC BY. Under CC BY-SA, yes, if you share the adapted work: it must use the same licence. GPL and CERN-OHL-S have a similar condition.

Does the model’s licence also cover the robot’s software?

Not necessarily. The design and the software often have separate licences; Otto DIY, for example, has its design under CC BY-SA 4.0 and its software under GPL-3.0. Always read both.

May a school print NC-licensed parts for teaching?

Non-commercial teaching usually falls under NC, but the specific situation matters, for example whether people pay for the course. If unsure, ask the author; the author’s consent always settles it.
